# The Bloop Times — content playbook

Everything that goes into an edition, and how to fill it without ever
getting caught out. Read this once; after that, Friday is a routine.

**The promise, printed on the site and in every welcome email:**

> The funniest bloopers, untold behind-the-scenes stories, and the biggest
> movie updates — delivered straight to your inbox.

Three things. Every edition has to deliver all three, or the promise is a lie
and the $9.99 is a refund waiting to happen.

---

## The one rule

**Nothing goes in that you can't point at a source for.**

A blooper channel lives on trust: readers believe you because you know things
they don't. One invented casting rumour, one made-up release date, one "fun
fact" that turns out to be a Reddit myth, and that's gone. It doesn't come back.

So, for every claim:

| Section type | What counts as good enough |
|---|---|
| News (1, 2, 10) | Two trade outlets, or one plus the studio's own announcement. Deadline, Variety, THR, Collider, the studio press site. |
| Behind the scenes (3, 7, 8, 9, 14) | A named person saying it, in a named place: a commentary track, a podcast, a printed interview, a documentary, an official DVD extra. |
| Facts (5, 6) | A primary source. If the only trail leads to an aggregator or a listicle, drop it. |
| Bloopers (4) | The gag reel itself. If you can't see it, you can't describe it. |

If a story is *good but unverified*, you have two honest options: say so in the
text ("as the story goes, though nobody involved has confirmed it"), or leave
it out. Never a third.

When something turns out wrong: print the correction in the next edition, in
the Improvised or Scripted sidebar or under the lead. Corrections build more
trust than never being wrong.

## The running order

This is the spine. Same order every week, so readers learn where things are.

| # | Section | Length | Always? |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **The Front Page** — the biggest movie news | 300–400 words | Every week |
| 2 | **The Wire** — three short movie/actor updates | 3 × 45–70 words | Every week |
| 3 | **Behind the Scenes** — the long story | 400–500 words | Every week |
| 4 | **Blooper of the Week** — the fact sheet | 4 rows + 3 sentences | Every week |
| 5 | **Improvised or Scripted?** — one line, settled | 150–200 words | Every week |
| 6 | **Did You Know?** — three facts | 3 × 1–2 sentences | Every week |
| — | **The rotating slot** | 150–250 words | One of three, see below |
| 7 | **Coming Soon** — releases | 5 rows | Every week |
| 8 | **Reader Poll** + **Guess the Movie** | short | Every week |
| 9 | **Video of the Week** + Fan Corner | 100 words | Every week |
| 10 | **Insiders Only** — the subscriber story | 300–400 words | Every week |
| 11 | **What We're Working On** — next Friday | 3 lines | Every week |

Total: roughly 1,600–2,000 words. About a 9-minute read. Long enough to feel
like a paper, short enough to actually finish over coffee.

### The rotating slot

Sits between Did You Know? and Coming Soon. One of these three, on a
four-week cycle:

| Week | Slot |
|---|---|
| 1 | Deleted Scene of the Week |
| 2 | Casting That Almost Happened |
| 3 | Where Are They Now? |
| 4 | Deleted Scene of the Week (or whichever has the best story that week) |

Rotating keeps it fresh and keeps you from scraping the barrel on any one of
them. Don't force it — if week 2 has a brilliant Where Are They Now? and a
weak casting story, swap them.

## Section by section

### 1 · The Front Page — the biggest movie news
The week's actual biggest movie story, told the way a blooper paper would tell
it. Not a press release. Lead with what happened, then find the angle only you
have: the reshoot nobody mentioned, the actor who's broken on set before, the
production that's already been through this.

**Never** invent a detail to make the angle work. If the angle isn't there,
pick a different story.

### 2 · The Wire — three short updates
Two industry items, one actor item. 45–70 words each, one sourced fact per
item. The actor item is "Actor Update of the Week": what they signed, wrapped
or said this week, plus the blooper they're quietly famous for. That second
half is what makes it yours.

### 3 · Behind the Scenes — the long story
The section people actually pay for. One scene, one take, one production — told
properly, with a beginning and a payoff. Aim for a story a fan of the film has
*not* heard. If you'd have to explain why it's interesting, it isn't.

Structure that works: set the scene → what happened → the quote → how it ended
up in (or out of) the film → the landing. The sidebar box carries "the
receipts": the commentary track, the interview, the documentary.

### 4 · Blooper of the Week — the fact sheet
Four fixed rows, always in this order:

1. **The film** — title, year, director
2. **What went wrong** — the mistake, plainly
3. **Why they laughed** — the timing, the reaction, who broke first
4. **Did it stay in?** — yes or no, and what the final cut shows instead

Row 4 is the punchline. Never skip it. Then two or three sentences of colour —
what the take sounded like, how long the crew was down — and the video link.

### 5 · Improvised or Scripted?
One famous line. What everyone assumes, what the evidence says, then the stamp:
IMPROVISED, SCRIPTED, or BOTH (a scripted line the actor reshaped). Then the
source — who confirmed it, where, when. The sidebar keeps a running list of
what you've settled before, which quietly proves the archive is worth having.

BOTH is a legitimate verdict and often the true one. Use it.

### 6 · Did You Know? — three facts
Three, never more. One or two sentences each, each one checkable. Bias towards
production facts over trivia: how a shot was achieved beats which actor is
tallest. If a fact needs a paragraph to land, it's a Behind the Scenes story,
not a Did You Know.

### 7 · Deleted Scene of the Week *(rotating)*
A scene that was shot and cut. What was in it, and — the important half — *why*
it went. Studio note, pacing, test screening, ratings. Where it can be seen now,
if it can.

### 8 · Casting That Almost Happened *(rotating)*
Who nearly had the part, how close it got, and why it didn't happen. Be careful
here: "was considered for" and "was offered and turned it down" are very
different claims. Say which one you have.

### 9 · Where Are They Now? *(rotating)*
Someone from a film readers know, and what they're doing now. Keep it warm.
Never punch down at a former child actor, and never speculate about someone's
health, finances, or private life. If the honest answer is "they left the
industry and would rather not be written about," pick someone else.

### 10 · Coming Soon — releases
Five rows: date, title, where, one line on why you'll care. Mix cinema and
streaming. **Re-check every single week** — release dates move constantly, and
a wrong date is the easiest possible way to look careless. Print the date you
checked.

### 11 · Reader Poll
One question, four options, linked to a beehiiv poll or a YouTube community
post. Then last week's result as bars, plus one line of commentary. The
commentary is what makes the poll worth reading rather than clicking.

### 12 · Guess the Movie
A deliberately unhelpful cropped still and five clues, hardest first. Answer
printed upside down (on the web) or in small grey type at the bottom of the box
(in email), together with last week's answer.

### 13 · BloopUniverse Video of the Week
The week's upload, plus something readers can't get from the thumbnail: why you
made it, what nearly didn't make the cut, the moment worth waiting for. This is
the section that turns readers into viewers.

### 14 · Insiders Only — the subscriber story
The single most important section in the paper. A story that never made a video
and never will — because it's too small, too odd, or works better written down.

If an Insider could have got this from the channel, from a Short, or from a
Google search, it does not belong here. Ask that question every week before you
publish. This section is the answer to "why am I paying $9.99?"

### 15 · What We're Working On
Three lines: next week's lead, the video in the edit, next week's rotating
slot. Then the invitation to reply. It gives readers a reason to open next
Friday, and the replies feed Fan Corner.

## The weekly rhythm

Spreading it across the week beats one panicked Thursday night.

| Day | Job |
|---|---|
| **Monday** | Scan the trades. Pick the front page and the three Wire items. Note sources as you go — chasing them back later costs an hour. |
| **Tuesday** | Write Behind the Scenes and the rotating slot. These take the longest; do them while you're fresh. |
| **Wednesday** | Blooper of the Week, Improvised or Scripted, Did You Know. Cut the Guess the Movie still. |
| **Thursday** | Coming Soon (re-check every date), poll results, Fan Corner from the week's comments, Video of the Week, the Insiders-only story. |
| **Friday morning** | Fact-check pass, then publish: web edition first, then send from beehiiv. |

**The fact-check pass, in order:**

1. Every name spelled right — actors, directors, films
2. Every year and every release date
3. Every claim in sections 1, 2 and 10 traceable to its source
4. Every link opens, and goes where the text says it goes
5. Vol./No. bumped in the topbar *and* the footer
6. Send yourself a test email and read it on your phone

## Publishing an edition

**Web**

1. `cp -r template/ editions/YYYY-MM-DD/` and rename to `index.html`
2. Fill every `[PLACEHOLDER]`
3. Add the edition to the top of `archive.html`
4. Add the URL to `sitemap.xml`
5. Commit and push — Cloudflare publishes within a minute

**Email**

1. Open `emails/weekly-template.html`, fill every `[PLACEHOLDER]`
2. beehiiv → new post → Blank draft → `/` → Custom HTML → paste
3. Set the audience to **Premium only** so it goes to Insiders
4. Subject and preview text (see below), send yourself a test, publish

**Subject lines that work:** the specific, strange detail, not the section
name. "The Thor line that came from a nine-year-old" beats "The Bloop Times
No. 7". Keep it under 45 characters so phones don't cut it.

**Preview text:** never repeat the subject. Use it to add the second hook.

## The welcome email is a different animal

`emails/welcome-free-edition.html` is not a Friday edition. It is sent
automatically the moment somebody signs up, which means it might be read the
day it was written or eleven months later.

**So it carries no news.** No front page, no Wire, no Coming Soon, no view
counts, no "this week". Only the evergreen sections: Behind the Scenes,
Blooper of the Week, Improvised or Scripted?, Did You Know?, one rotating
slot, Guess the Movie, and three archive videos.

The clever part is that the email *says so*, in a section near the end called
"You may have noticed there's no news in it". The three sections it's missing
are exactly the three you get on Friday. The limitation is the pitch.

Two rules when you refresh it:

- **Nothing dated.** No release dates, no subscriber counts, no view counts,
  no "last week". If it would age, it doesn't go in.
- **Every story sourced in the text.** The italic source line under each story
  is doing real work: it's the reason a stranger believes the next one, and
  belief is what they're being asked to pay for.

Refresh it maybe twice a year, or whenever a story in it stops being your
best. Anyone who signed up before the change keeps the old one — that's fine,
they already got a complete paper.

## Tone

It's a newspaper about people getting things wrong, written by someone who
loves the films. Dry, precise, quietly funny. The jokes live in the captions,
the picture credits and the running gags — the reporting itself stays straight.

Punch up at studios and budgets, never down at actors. Nobody reads a blooper
paper to watch someone get humiliated; they read it for the half-second where a
$200m superhero remembers they're a person.
