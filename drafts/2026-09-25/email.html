Digger Held Its Royal Premiere. Its Own Reviewers Couldn't Agree On It.
Tom Cruise's strangest role in years got a red carpet with Prince William on it — and three verdicts from three critics at the same magazine.
By The Bloop Times Editorial Desk

Alejandro González Iñárritu's Digger, starring Tom Cruise as oil tycoon Digger Rockwell, held its world premiere in London on Tuesday night as this year's Royal Film Performance, with Prince William and Princess Catherine seated in the front row for a screening whose proceeds go to The Film and TV Charity. Cruise called the role "totally outrageous," telling reporters afterward, "I've never read a character like it. I've never read a film like this."

What nobody could settle on afterward was whether the film is any good. The official review embargo doesn't lift until Sept. 29, but critics who saw it this week were cleared to post first reactions — and IndieWire alone sent three of its own writers to the same screening and published three verdicts that flatly disagree with each other.

  "A self-indulgent miscalculation from end to end."

That's Ryan Lattanzio's read, printed in the same magazine where colleague Anne Thompson called the film "a brave and gorgeous art film" and Jim Hemphill called it "one of the most visually inventive, conceptually audacious, and unpredictable studio movies ever made." Other outlets describe the same split running through the wider field, with reactions ranging from "audacious" and "masterpiece" to "hollow misfire" and "insufferable." A film that can't get three writers at one magazine to agree isn't going to be boring to have an opinion about.

Sources: Deadline, Variety, IndieWire, The Hollywood Reporter · Sept. 22–23, 2026

Behind the Scenes · The story you weren't told
The Burp In Elf Was Never Will Ferrell's
Twenty-three years on, Buddy the Elf's most-quoted sound effect still isn't the one most people think it is.

In Elf (2003), Buddy sits down to his first family dinner in New York, drains an entire two-liter bottle of Coca-Cola, and lets out a roughly 12-second burp that has outlasted most of the rest of the film in cultural memory — re-clipped, quoted and re-uploaded for two decades. What almost nobody clocks watching it: the sound itself isn't Will Ferrell's.

Ferrell performed the scene physically — the posture, the face, the slow-motion horror of his onscreen family — but the belch was dubbed in afterward by Maurice LaMarche, the voice actor behind The Brain in Pinky and the Brain and Kif in Futurama. Ferrell's own on-set attempt didn't have the operatic scale director Jon Favreau wanted for the joke, so LaMarche was brought in separately, in a different recording session, to supply what the scene actually needed.

  "That's my stunt burp in there — not his."

LaMarche has confirmed the swap himself, on The Ship-It Show podcast and in a People TV interview, describing the four-figure residual check that first stopped him in his tracks — and the smaller ones that still arrive every December.

The film's own sound team backs him up from the other side of the desk. Wade Wilson, Elf's head sound designer and now a Sound Design faculty member at the University of North Carolina School of the Arts, has walked students through how the belch was layered and shaped in post until it stopped sounding like biology and started sounding like a punchline.

The burp made the final cut precisely as engineered — one of the rare entries in this column that isn't a mistake at all. It's a special effect built to sound like one, and it's been fooling family movie night for over two decades.

Told by Maurice LaMarche, voice actor, on The Ship-It Show podcast and in a People TV interview; and by Wade Wilson, the film's head sound designer, in a University of North Carolina School of the Arts interview.

 
Blooper of the Week
The Hero Isabela Merced Almost Saved Was The Wrong One
The film
Superman (2025), dir. James Gunn
What went wrong
Being lowered by a rig into a mid-air fight scene as Hawkgirl, Isabela Merced flubbed her cue and called out the wrong hero entirely: "I almost hit Spider-Man, I mean, Superman!" — a hero from a rival studio's franchise, invoked mid-take in the middle of DC's biggest movie of the year.
Why they laughed
Hearing the cast of Warner Bros.' tentpole accidentally promote the competition, live, mid-rig, broke the take immediately — and made the moment one of the most re-clipped seconds in the whole gag reel once it went online.
Did it stay in?
No — gag reel only. Merced's Hawkgirl scenes made the finished film; this particular line never did.

The mix-up surfaced when the gag reel arrived with Superman's digital release on Sept. 23, 2025, alongside David Corenswet and Nicholas Hoult breaking character mid-scene, and Alan Tudyk — in motion-capture as the Fortress of Solitude robot "Gary" — staying so deadpan that he ended up breaking Corenswet instead.

Sources: Yahoo Entertainment, Nerdist, Bleeding Cool — Superman gag reel, released with the Sept. 23, 2025 digital edition.

 
Improvised or Scripted?
"You're gonna need a bigger boat."
Jaws (1975) — Chief Brody (Roy Scheider), getting his first real look at the shark.

This is the one everyone assumes was pure ad-lib: an actor, staring down a mechanical shark that barely worked, blurting out the obvious. The truth is closer to both.

BOTH

"You're gonna need a bigger boat" started as a real crew complaint. Producers Richard Zanuck and David Brown were, in screenwriter Carl Gottlieb's account, notoriously tight with the budget — so whenever something went wrong on the water, from a late lunch to swells rocking the camera boat, someone would mutter the line as a running joke. Gottlieb wrote a version of it into the script during rewrites. Roy Scheider then delivered it more than once during filming and, by his own account in a 2000 interview, wasn't fully sure himself whether the exact phrasing that made the final cut was scripted or something he landed on in the moment.

Where Are They Now?
Kevin McCallister, All Grown Up, Wants To Be The One Locked Out

Thirty-six years after Home Alone made him the most famous eight-year-old in America, Macaulay Culkin has been meeting with Disney executives about bringing Kevin McCallister back — not as the kid left behind, but as the parent who gets left out. Puck News reporter Matthew Belloni broke the story on his podcast, The Town: Culkin pitched Disney his own idea, with Kevin now a widower or divorced father, working too much, who comes home to find his own son has locked him out and booby-trapped the house against him.

TMZ confirmed the meeting took place at Disney's Burbank studio afterward, describing the project as still an early idea rather than a greenlit film — "an almost green light," in one description of where talks stand. It's a fitting update for the character: Kevin's whole appeal was always a kid outsmarting the adults in the house, and Culkin's pitch just swaps which side of that joke he'd be standing on.

Reported by Matthew Belloni on The Town (Puck News podcast); confirmed by TMZ, July 28, 2026. Nothing has been greenlit.

What "30 Films A Year" Actually Buys You (Hint: Maybe Nothing)

Buried in Monday's Paramount-Warner Bros. Discovery settlement is a number that sounds like great news for anyone who misses going to the movies: the merged studio must release 30 films a year for its first two years, then 32 a year for three more — 156 films over five years, or it starts writing $30 million checks into Hollywood union health and pension funds for every one it misses.

Here's the part that didn't make most headlines. Paramount and Warner Bros. are already on pace to release about 28 films a year combined — so the "new" commitment is barely two films above what's already happening. The last time a single studio released 30 movies in one year was 2007; today's major studios average roughly half that. One analyst's warning cuts right to it: "Protecting the number of films does not protect the money spent making them," Paolo Pescatore of PP Foresight told Reuters — a studio can hit 30 pictures a year and still shrink the budget behind every one to get there.

There's a precedent for this kind of promise falling short quietly. When Disney bought 21st Century Fox in 2019, the combined company had been releasing roughly 26 films a year; by 2025, per the same Reuters analysis, that had shrunk to just 14 wide releases. Nobody broke a contract that time — there wasn't one. This settlement at least attaches a number and a penalty. Whether 30 becomes real movies in real theaters, or a target lawyers can hit while spending less on each one, is the actual story — the one buried under every headline that just said the merger "clears a hurdle."
