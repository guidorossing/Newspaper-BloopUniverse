Spider-Man Just Had The Fastest Climb To $900 Million In Marvel History
Six straight weekends at No. 1, on a production that stopped cold a year ago when its star took a real hit to the head.
By The Bloop Times Editorial Desk

Spider-Man: Brand New Day became only the second movie this decade to hold No. 1 for six straight weekends, per Variety's Labor Day report, and on Sept. 7 it crossed $900 million domestic in 36 days — the fastest any film has done it, per Deadline. Worldwide it's taken in $2.4 billion, the third-biggest global total ever, behind only Avatar and Avengers: Endgame.

None of it looked guaranteed a year ago. In September 2025, production paused for a week at Warner Bros. Studios Leavesden after Holland was hospitalized with a mild concussion following a stunt, per The Hollywood Reporter and Variety. No one else was hurt, and the release date never moved — but it's a fair reminder that the actor whose gag reels usually show him laughing has just as often been the one getting hurt.

  "We're just adding the icing on the cake."

The reshoots that followed, Holland told GQ this spring, weren't about fixing a broken film — they were sharpening one that already worked. Six weeks at No. 1 suggests the icing worked.

Sources: Variety, Deadline · Sept. 2026 (box office); The Hollywood Reporter, Variety · Sept. 2025 (concussion); Deadline, via GQ · April 2026 (quote)

Behind the Scenes · The story you weren't told
The Bit Part That Invented The Movie Blooper Reel
Every blooper compilation on this channel is downstream of a decision one unknown Hong Kong stuntman made in 1982.
The tradition this whole channel runs on

In 1981, a mostly unknown Jackie Chan had a bit part in Hal Needham's cross-country comedy The Cannonball Run — the kind of cameo that vanishes from memory within a year. What didn't vanish was Needham's closing-credits gimmick: instead of a plain cast list, the film rolled its outtakes, actors flubbing lines and corpsing on camera.

Chan, by his own account, had never seen anything like it. A year later he wrote, directed and starred in Dragon Lord (1982), and became the first filmmaker to bring that idea into a Hong Kong action picture — running his own blown takes and botched stunts under his own credits.

  One bit part in someone else's movie. Four decades of imitators.

It stuck because it fit what Chan's films were selling: that the danger was real. A Hollywood star could lean on effects and doubles; Chan did his own falls, and the failed attempts backed up every successful one before it. By the 2000s the blooper reel wasn't an afterthought — distributors marketed it.

Which is the quiet debt this channel is still running on: every gag reel BloopUniverse has cut together, Marvel's included, follows a format one stuntman with a bit part borrowed from a Burt Reynolds car-chase comedy.

Jackie Chan's own account, as reported by IMDb, ScreenRant and NPR; origin dated to The Cannonball Run (1981) and Dragon Lord (1982).

 
Blooper of the Week
The Goat That Upstaged A Dying Avenger
The film
Avengers: Infinity War (2018), dir. Anthony & Joe Russo
What went wrong
T'Challa and Okoye are talking a reluctant Bucky Barnes into the fight against Thanos. Off camera, one of the Wakanda set's goats picked that exact moment to start bleating.
Why they laughed
Danai Gurira broke almost instantly. Chadwick Boseman held his Wakandan accent a few seconds longer, then turned to the goat mid-take: "Okay now, you are going too far now."
Did it stay in?
No — Blu-ray gag reel only. The theatrical scene plays with no interruptions and no goats.
Wakanda's throne room has never had a tougher heckler

The full gag reel runs about ninety seconds longer than the first 45-second preview Marvel released, and the goat gets its own round of applause in most of the fan compilations built around it since — one of the moments most cited when people talk about how real Infinity War's ensemble chemistry actually was.

Improvised or Scripted?
"You talkin' to me?"
Taxi Driver (1976) — Robert De Niro, alone in his apartment, rehearsing violence in a mirror

Everyone assumes it's pure De Niro, and they're right — the less-told part is why the line exists at all.

Improvised

Production was behind schedule and the studio wanted the scene wrapped. Scorsese kept the cameras rolling anyway. "We were behind schedule. We were in such trouble," he told Stephen Colbert. "They were banging on the door and I had to open the door and say, this is good, this is good. Two more minutes. One more take." De Niro built the line take by take, with no page to fall back on. Stay on schedule, Scorsese has said, and it never happens.

Martin Scorsese, on The Late Show with Stephen Colbert, January 2024.

Deleted Scene of the Week
The Thor Scene Marvel Cut To Save The Farm

Avengers: Age of Ultron (2015) was supposed to have Thor disappear mid-film into a cave, sink into a pool of water, and get possessed by a Norn who answers Erik Selvig's questions through him — a detour meant to explain the visions pulling Thor toward Ragnarok.

It tested badly. Director Joss Whedon has said the rough cut, shown without finished effects, played as a strange left turn in an ensemble movie — a scene that would work beautifully in a standalone Thor film and nowhere else. Marvel wanted it gone entirely; Whedon fought to keep fragments, describing the negotiation to Collider bluntly: "They pointed a gun at the farm's head and said, give us the cave, or we'll take out the farm" — the farmhouse sequence being what he traded to keep it.

Sources: Collider, The Hollywood Reporter, SlashFilm — Whedon interviews and the deleted scene's home-video release, 2015.

Why Doomsday's Best Rumor Didn't Make The Wire

If you follow Marvel scoopers, you already know more about Doomsday's reshoots than made this week's Wire item. Tipster accounts have spent weeks naming names: a returning Scarlet Witch, specific new cameo casting. Some of it is probably true. None of it has a name attached who'd answer for it if wrong.

That's why the Wire item sticks to what the Russos told Deadline on the record, and leaves the scooper material out — even though it's the more exciting version, and even though plenty of readers have likely seen it shared as fact online. Two trade outlets, or one plus the studio's own word, isn't a formality. It's the difference between reporting the cast and guessing it.

So here's the actual insider information: treat nothing about Doomsday's cast as confirmed until Marvel or the Russos say it themselves. Not a thrilling scoop. But it's the whole reason you're paying for this instead of scrolling for it.
