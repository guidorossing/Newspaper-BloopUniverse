Spider-Man Just Became The Highest-Grossing Movie In U.S. Box Office History
Brand New Day passed Star Wars: The Force Awakens on its 47th day in theaters, ending a decade-long record — and the film that broke it still has no gag reel to show for it.
By The Bloop Times Editorial Desk

Spider-Man: Brand New Day crossed $936.773 million domestically on Sept. 16, per Variety, Deadline and The Hollywood Reporter — enough to edge past the $936.6 million Star Wars: The Force Awakens had held as the biggest domestic release ever since 2015. Worldwide, per TheWrap, the film has taken in $2.45 billion, and Sony says it's the fastest release ever to reach every domestic milestone from $400 million through $900 million.

It happened without a single new stunt or set story making headlines this week — just a seventh straight weekend of ticket sales. Sony chairman Tom Rothman, speaking at Variety's Lumiere Summit, called the run "miraculous," crediting an industry still recovering from a pandemic and two strikes with turning up anyway.

  "Just think of everything our industry has been through with the pandemic, and then even more so with the strikes."

It leaves this paper in an odd spot. No Way Home, the last Spider-Man to set records like this, got its own gag reel five months after it opened — the Garfield-Maguire hug this paper ran last edition. Brand New Day is still in theaters, still selling, and has no gag reel yet. The biggest domestic release in history, and there's nothing to cut until Sony calls it done in cinemas.

Sources: Variety, Deadline, The Hollywood Reporter, TheWrap · Sept. 16, 2026 (record); Variety · Sept. 2026 (Rothman quote, Lumiere Summit)

Behind the Scenes · The story you weren't told
The Take That Nearly Killed Resident Evil's Star — And Made The Final Cut
Zach Cregger didn't want visual effects. So a 150-pound bag of fake blood came down on a crane, and missed Austin Abrams by inches.

Resident Evil, Zach Cregger's reboot of the zombie franchise, opens Friday. Its centerpiece sends Austin Abrams's character, a medical courier named Bryan, sprinting down a snowbound street while infected bodies drop from the rooftops on either side and detonate on landing.

Cregger didn't want the falls done digitally — he thought it would read as fake — so the crew rigged body-sized bags of blood, roughly 150 pounds each, to drop from cranes on cue as Abrams ran an obstacle course beneath them, camera car tracking him. One bag triggered early.

  "It would've killed him. If he had been one step to the right, he would've died."

That's Cregger's own account, given to journalists at a Q&A after an early screening. The bag missed Abrams by inches — and the take, near miss and all, made the finished cut, because the fear on his face was real. It wasn't the only close call: Cregger has also said Abrams split his head open in an elevator shaft during a separate accident on set.

Abrams has said the set pushed the cast toward instinct over scripted dialogue: "we improvised a lot of fucks, for sure," he told one interviewer, with Cregger explaining that fight-or-flight strips a scene to its most primal responses. A studio movie that nearly killed its own lead to get one shot right isn't a story the marketing has led with. It's the reason this paper wanted to see it in a theater, not on streaming.

Told by Zach Cregger at a press Q&A following an early Resident Evil screening, reported by SlashFilm, Deadline and The Playlist, July 2026; Austin Abrams quote from press interviews with Complex and JoBlo, 2026.

The S'more Joke That Never Made Deadpool & Wolverine
The film
Deadpool & Wolverine (2024), dir. Shawn Levy
What went wrong
At the film's closing campfire scene, gathered with Logan, Laura and the rest of the cast, Ryan Reynolds swapped in an ad-libbed line: Deadpool announcing that "Blade won't admit it, but he wants a s'more" — a non sequitur about Wesley Snipes's character, who is nowhere near the fire.
Why they laughed
The campfire scene closes the film on a quiet, earned beat between Logan and Laura. The cast broke watching Reynolds hijack it for a joke about camping snacks aimed at the one Marvel veteran who wasn't there to answer for himself.
Did it stay in?
No — gag reel only. The campfire scene is in the finished film; Blade's s'more is not.

The gag reel arrived with the film's digital release on Oct. 1, 2024, and also runs through Reynolds improvising alternate middle names for Tony Stark and Wolverine flipping off the camera, per CBR and ScreenRant. The s'more line is the one that keeps getting re-clipped, mostly because Blade had no idea he was being talked about.

Improvised or Scripted?
"Hey, Malkovich! Think fast!"
Being John Malkovich (1999) — a passing motorist beans John Malkovich with a thrown beer can

It's one of the most-repeated "did that really happen" stories about the film: that a drunk extra actually beaned Malkovich with a beer can, ad-libbed the line, and got a pay bump for it. Most of that is true. The part that surprises people is that none of it was an accident to begin with.

Scripted

Charlie Kaufman's shooting script, in circulation for years, has the beat written in black and white: a motorist leans out of a passing car and shouts "Hey, Malkovich! Think fast!" before a beer can hits him on the head. What's confirmed is that the film matches the page exactly. What's repeated everywhere but sourced nowhere specific is the detail about the extra's pay rise — so this paper isn't printing that part as fact.

Charlie Kaufman's published Being John Malkovich screenplay (dailyscript.com), checked against the finished film.

Casting That Almost Happened
The Bond Dick Van Dyke Turned Down — Because Of His Own Accent

When Sean Connery walked away from James Bond in the late 1960s, producer Cubby Broccoli went looking for a replacement. Dick Van Dyke, ahead of his 100th birthday this December, told NBC's Al Roker last November that Broccoli asked him directly: "Would you like to be Bond?"

Van Dyke turned it down on the spot, and his reasoning is the good part: "Have you heard my British accent?" — a nod to his own widely mocked Cockney turn as Bert in Mary Poppins (1964), one of the most gently ridiculed accents in movie history. He's said since that he regrets it, though he stands by the reasoning. With this week's Bond search back in the headlines (see The Wire), it's a reminder that the studio has been here before, and the actor they wanted once said no because he couldn't do the voice.

Sources: NBC's Today with Al Roker, Nov. 2025, as reported by IMDb, HuffPost and Yahoo Entertainment.

Why We're Not Calling It: Jack Lowden Isn't Confirmed As Bond

If you follow Bond scoopers even loosely, you already know the going theory: Jack Lowden, Gary Oldman's own Slow Horses co-star, has it. Oldman himself said so on a Canadian broadcast — "They have picked him" — and Polymarket's odds on Lowden jumped from 3.5% to 55% within days.

Except Oldman walked it back inside the same week, on stage at TIFF, saying plainly he doesn't actually know who was picked, that there are six names on a shortlist, and that his "fingers crossed for Jack" got reported as inside information it never was. The correction ran in the same outlet, Deadline, as the original claim — and got a fraction of the pickup.

So The Wire this week reports only what's confirmed: a shortlist exists, a favorite has emerged in the betting markets, and nobody with the authority to announce it has said a word. That's a thinner story than "Jack Lowden is Bond." It's also the only version of it that's true right now, and the only one this paper is willing to put a byline under.
