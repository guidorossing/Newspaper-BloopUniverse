The Nine-Year-Old Who Wrote Marvel's Best Joke
One of the most quoted lines in the entire MCU wasn't written by Marvel. It wasn't written by the director. It wasn't even written by Chris Hemsworth.

You know the moment. Thor is shoved into an arena on a garbage planet, braced for whatever horror comes through the gate — and out walks the Hulk. His face changes completely. He turns to a crowd that wants him dead and beams: "He's a friend from work!"

It's the biggest laugh in Thor: Ragnarok, and for years people assumed it was pure Hemsworth improv. It wasn't. There was a child on set that day, visiting through Make-A-Wish, and between takes he and Hemsworth got talking.

  "And he goes: you know, you should say he's a friend from work."

That's Hemsworth's own account of it. He tried the line on the next take. It stayed. It went into the trailer, then into the film, then into permanent circulation as one of the most repeated things anyone says in a Marvel movie.

There's a reason it could happen at all. Taika Waititi ran that set loose on purpose, pushing the cast to improvise most of their dialogue rather than hold the script. A tighter production would have thanked the kid politely and shot the scene as written.

Which is the thing about mistakes and accidents in film. Some of them aren't failures at all. Some of them are just what happens when somebody leaves the door open.

Told by Chris Hemsworth in press interviews for Thor: Ragnarok, 2017. The boy has never been publicly named.

 
Blooper of the Week
Twelve Ruined Takes That Became The Scene
The police line-up in The Usual Suspects is one of the most rewatched scenes of the nineties. Every laugh in it is real, and none of it was supposed to be there.
The film
The Usual Suspects (1995), dir. Bryan Singer
What went wrong
The line-up was written to be played stone-faced and menacing. Benicio del Toro spent the day breaking wind. Kevin Pollak's count: roughly twelve takes in a row.
Why they laughed
Five actors, one small room, an entire day of it, and a scene that demanded they look like hardened criminals. Nobody stood a chance.
Did it stay in?
Yes — the ruined takes are the scene. Singer gave up on the serious version and cut the funniest ones together. Screenwriter Christopher McQuarrie has since pointed out that it quietly solved a problem he'd been fighting: the film needed a moment where these five men felt like a crew, and no written scene had managed it.

Watch it again knowing that, and it changes. Those aren't five criminals sizing each other up. That's five actors losing a fight they'd been losing all afternoon.

 
Improvised or Scripted?
"You're gonna need a bigger boat."
Jaws (1975) — Roy Scheider, the first time anyone gets a proper look at the shark

It isn't in the script. Scheider ad-libbed it. But he didn't invent it either — and the real story is better than the myth.

Improvised

The shoot was on open water and, per co-writer Carl Gottlieb, the producers were being stingy. The crew worked off a barge with a support boat everyone agreed was too small. So it became the joke: lunch is late, you're gonna need a bigger boat. The swell is ruining the shot, you're gonna need a bigger boat. Anything goes wrong, same line.

Scheider heard it all summer and started slipping it into takes. One of them landed at the exact moment the audience finally sees what they've been dreading for an hour. It stayed. It is now, arguably, the most famous piece of on-set griping in cinema.

Carl Gottlieb, co-writer of Jaws, to The Hollywood Reporter, 2016.

 
Did You Know?
Three Footnotes To The Above
1 The Jaws shark was named Bruce, after Spielberg's lawyer. It worked perfectly in the warehouse and failed the moment it touched seawater — on day one it sank to the bottom of Nantucket Sound. Salt water corroded the motor within a week.
2 Because the shark kept breaking, Spielberg had to shoot around it — less monster, more suspense. The single most praised thing about Jaws is a workaround for a machine that didn't work.
3 The Ragnarok line only got in because Waititi had the cast improvising most of their dialogue. On a normal Marvel set, a visitor's suggestion never reaches the film.

Casting That Almost Happened
The Man Who Said No To Neo

In 1997 the Wachowskis offered Will Smith the lead in The Matrix. He passed. He took Wild Wild West instead.

Fresh off Men in Black, he was worried about becoming, in his words, the alien movie guy. So he heard the pitch — a pitch that involved a great deal of describing what bullet time would look like once it existed — and said no.

The Matrix took $463.5 million and four Academy Awards. Wild Wild West took $222.1 million and a critical mauling.

To his credit, Smith has never dressed it up. He laid the whole thing out himself in a video on his own channel in 2019: "This is one of them stories I'm not proud of, but it's the truth." And in 2025, twenty-eight years late, he finally played Neo — in one of his own music videos.

Will Smith, on his own YouTube channel, 2019. Box office: worldwide gross.
